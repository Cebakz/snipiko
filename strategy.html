SNITRIX / FIELD NOTES

A little room goes a long way

Good Snitrix moves do two jobs: place the current clue and leave the board useful for the next one. These examples show what to look for.

Updated November 25, 2026
On this pageCompare orientationsKeep useful spacePlan the next clearUnderstand the scoreBefore you release

Compare the shape, not just the number

Area stays the same when you rotate a rectangle, but the empty space you leave behind changes. Before committing a 6, compare 3 × 2 and 2 × 3. Use a long 1 × 6 only when there is a full clear path and it does not trap another clue.

Start with constrained clues. A clue against an edge, a large number with few possible shapes, or a clue between blocks can have fewer useful options. Placing a flexible small clue first may take away the only route for a larger one.

Empty squares are not all equally useful

Twelve scattered empty squares do not necessarily fit a rectangle with area 6. Shape and connectivity matter. Keep a few connected rectangular areas rather than many isolated holes.

A 1 can fill an awkward single square, but placing it into an open area may divide that area. A 2 can bridge a useful gap or close off a narrow lane. Check neighboring clues before treating small numbers as automatic moves.

Watch NEXT without assuming its landing square.

The queue shows values. Reserve shapes that could accommodate those values, but do not plan as though you know exactly where the clues will arrive.

Set up a clear that gives room back

Look for rows and columns with only one small gap. Then check whether a clue can legally cover that gap. A nearly full row is useful only when its missing shape is reachable.

Classic lets you think without a countdown. If the combo permits a setup move, use that move to create a specific next clear. After a clear, reassess the remaining board: the best plan may have changed.

Blitz 5 Minutes adds a different constraint: a five-minute active-play clock. It refills clues after moves, so balance planning with the time remaining. Journey and Daily Challenge ask you to meet the displayed objective within a move budget. A high-scoring move is not always the right move for that objective.

Compare a move that clears one row and one column with a move that clears neither in the two-line workshop exercise.

Understand Classic scoring

The current Classic scoring gives 20 points per placed square. Line points depend on how many lines the move clears and on the combo. An All Clear adds a flat 1,500-point bonus. These worked examples use a combo of 1 and describe scoring only; they are not promises about the next board.

Example moveCalculationTotal
A 6, no line cleared6 × 20120
A 3 clears one row3 × 20 + 250 × 1²310
A 4 clears one row and one column4 × 20 + 250 × 2²1,080
That same two-line move also clears the board1,080 + 1,5002,580

At higher combos, line points receive the Classic combo multiplier. Its line multiplier is capped at combo 5, with additional tiers at combo 5 and 10. The visible combo counter can continue beyond that cap. The All Clear bonus stays flat.

The aim is to keep making useful moves. A smaller clear that preserves the board can lead to a longer run than a large score increase that leaves no room. No strategy guarantees an endless game.

A quick check before you release

  1. One clue? Your rectangle must contain exactly one number.
  2. Right area? Count width × height, including the clue’s own square.
  3. Clear path? Check every square for an existing block.
  4. Useful afterward? Look at the holes, remaining clues and lines your move will leave.
  5. What comes next? Check the NEXT values before placing your rectangle. In Blitz 5 Minutes, decide how much of the remaining clock you can spend comparing fits.

When no clear plan stands out, compare two legal orientations before using a hint. Learning why one shape leaves more room is often more useful than placing it quickly.
